The Leaf Is a Crossover Now: Nissan Prices the 2026 Redesign From $29,990

SO
Shedrack Olisa
September 12, 2026 · 6 min read

The Nissan Leaf has spent fifteen years being the affordable electric car that everyone respected and comparatively few people bought. For its third generation, Nissan has stopped defending the hatchback and rebuilt the Leaf as a subcompact crossover, priced from $29,990. It is the most commercially sensible thing the company could have done, even if it costs the car some of its character.

Why the Shape Changed

The outgoing Leaf was a hatchback in a market that had comprehensively stopped buying hatchbacks. The new car is a subcompact crossover with a lower drag coefficient than before, which is the rare case of a taller body arriving alongside better aerodynamics rather than in spite of them.

This is not a styling exercise. Crossovers are where the volume is, and an efficient electric powertrain matters far less commercially than a shape buyers will actually walk into a showroom for.

Pricing and Trims

Three trims, and the entry price is the headline. Nissan has held the Leaf under $30,000 at a moment when the $7,500 federal purchase credit is gone and every rival's effective price has jumped by exactly that amount.

The 52 kWh entry car pairs with a 174 hp motor. Step up to the 75 kWh pack and you get 214 hp and up to 303 miles of range. For most households the larger battery is the one worth stretching for, but the cheaper car is not a decoy trim, and that matters.

Charging, and the NACS Question

The new Leaf charges at up to 150 kW and, more importantly, has dual charging ports including native NACS. That means Tesla Supercharger access without carrying an adapter, which for a car aimed at first-time EV buyers removes one of the most persistent sources of confusion.

Nissan has also reworked the battery thermal management to charge better in cold weather. Anyone who owned an early Leaf will recognise why that sentence carries weight: the first-generation car's passively cooled pack was its defining engineering weakness, and cold-weather charging was where it hurt most.

Worth knowing: Native NACS plus revised thermal management addresses the two complaints that followed the Leaf for over a decade. If you dismissed the nameplate years ago on charging grounds, this is a different proposition.

Inside

The cabin gets a 12.3-inch display on the S+ and 14.3-inch screens on SV+ and Platinum+, along with a flat floor under the dashboard and redesigned seats that free up second-row knee room. None of that is remarkable in 2026, which is rather the point. The Leaf no longer asks to be judged as an electric car; it asks to be judged as a small crossover.

The Timing

Launching an affordable EV into the post-credit US market looks either brave or shrewd depending on the quarter you examine. US EV sales fell sharply after the federal credit expired, and the cheapest models were hit hardest because the $7,500 represented a far larger share of their price.

That is precisely the gap the new Leaf is aimed at. With nothing to subsidise, the winning move is to build a car that is genuinely cheap rather than one that was cheap only after a rebate. A $29,990 crossover with 150 kW charging and a NACS port is a more honest offer than a $37,000 car that used to cost $29,500 on paper.
